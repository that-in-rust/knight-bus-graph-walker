## Product Conclusions

This partition primarily examines pricing, procurement, migration testimony and vendor positioning. It supplies no new measured low-RAM architecture result. Repeated analyses often reach opposite verdicts from the same underlying material; count customers and primary events, not review iterations. The useful product question is which recurring customer job is worth switching for, not whether a large vendor bill alone proves a market.

### Ranked Opportunities

These are workload-discovery priorities and hypotheses, not a reduction of the parent goal or measured feasibility claims.

1. **Recurring snapshot analytics with an owned deadline.** An analytics/data-platform owner wants repeatable results from CSV or Neo4j without sizing the main database for analytical peaks. On-demand-memory and persistence-cost claims make this worth testing, but ephemeral incumbent sessions already compete. Ask freshness tolerance, queries per prepared snapshot, refresh frequency, time to first complete answer and what decision the result changes. Evidence: P2726-P2809, P2989-P3027, P3563-P3611.
2. **Departmental analysis where paid licensing and transition effort dominate.** The small-graph departmental account couples affordability with Cypher compatibility and a paid replacement. A simple supported workflow may win even when the graph already fits RAM; low-RAM engineering is not established as its purchase trigger. Verify actual properties/bytes, procedures, write needs, UI/export workflow and migration effort. Evidence: P3119-P3208, P3800-P3842.
3. **Performance-constrained analytics where cost helps authorize migration.** Technical-trigger testimony and large-graph scaling complaints suggest a buyer who needs a job completed reliably before caring about a cheaper bill. Require the exact query family, filters, weights, output semantics, convergence and refresh workload; storage/query speed alone is insufficient. Evidence: P3119-P3208, P3657-P3705. These examples do not establish that their datasets fit the50-GB prepared-storage cap.
4. **Enterprise analytical worker alongside a retained source.** A platform owner can evaluate a contained analytical job without replacing transactions, security and availability. Procurement shows potential spend but not the portion this worker removes. Enterprise procurement is not synonymous with dedicated hosting, and a smaller worker might not change a committed license at all. Evidence: P2810-P2988, P3706-P3799, P3892-P3936.
5. **Workflow simplification that removes an unnecessary graph service.** The Dgraph and Neptune migration accounts are useful counterexamples: a changed application or relational solution may beat a better graph engine. Include those alternatives when graph semantics are not essential; do not count them as Neo4j churn. Evidence: P3119-P3162, P3430-P3472.
